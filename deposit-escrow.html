Vietnam security deposit: crypto, VietQR, and why escrow usually isn’t a thing

People search “crypto escrow” because they do not want to hand two months of rent in cash and hope. In Vietnam the working version is simpler: pay VND into the account named on the lease, and keep the bank record.

There is no national deposit escrow

A HCMC or Hanoi lease usually asks for one to two months’ rent as a security deposit, paid before keys. The money sits with the owner or the agency. There is no government-held tenant scheme, no default lawyer escrow, and no landlord who will lock USDT in a smart contract until you both click “release.”

That is the gap the search is really about. You want a third party to hold the cash so it comes back. They want dong in a Vietnamese bank, on move-in day, from a name they can match to the contract. Those are different jobs. Pretending a wallet is an escrow agent does not make it one.

What “crypto escrow” gets wrong here

Do not send USDT, USDC, or any coin to the landlord “to hold.” Most will not. The ones who will have no lease-shaped way to return it, and you have no incoming-transfer line in a Vietnamese bank when you leave.

Do not park the deposit with a Telegram money guy or a Facebook “escrow admin.” Fast, informal, and useless when the fight is “which account received two months.”

Do not mix currencies. If the contract is in VND, pay VND. Paying USD cash or a USD quote that nobody locked in writing is how the refund becomes an FX argument.

A real third-party hold — a law firm, a licensed agency client account — exists for some villas and corporate leases. For a normal apartment it is rare. Ask once. If they say no, you are back to a bank transfer and a contract, not a protocol.

The record that actually matters

When you leave, the dispute is almost never “did crypto work.” It is “did you pay two months, into which account, and what was deducted for paint.” The document Vietnamese landlords already understand is a normal incoming transfer in their banking app, with a memo they can read.

So the practical stack is: amount in VND written on the lease, account name matching the person or company on the contract, VietQR for that account, transfer content like “ASH 12A deposit.” Screenshot the receipt. Owner vs agency

The deposit often goes to a different QR than rent. Agencies collect into a company account. Confirm the name on the QR against the contract before you send. Do not reuse last month’s rent QR for the deposit “because it worked.”

Utilities are not the deposit

Electricity, water, and internet are frequently a building QR. Paying those does not prove you paid the security deposit.

Cash still works — it just vanishes

Older owners want bills. Get a handwritten receipt with the VND amount and both names. Do not argue product at the door.

Paying the deposit from crypto

If you already hold digital dollars or crypto and you do not have a Vietnamese bank, the clean path is: scan their VietQR, send the VND figure on the lease, they see dong. They never open a wallet. VietQR is the rail foreigners actually use.

Getting it back

Pay the deposit on the same rail you will use for rent. Keep the contract, the QR screenshot, and the incoming-transfer receipt. Photograph the place on move-in. Deduction fights are about condition, not the rail.
